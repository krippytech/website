Technical Case · KT-000007

Repeated Scanner Problems Exposed an Aging Workstation

A scanner ticket briefly improved after workstation cleanup, but the same workflow failed again within days while the machine remained pinned at 100% CPU and every click could take 10 to 20 seconds.The decision changed from “repair the scanner” to “stop spending time on a workstation that no longer restores confidence.”

Documented by Michael Miller · Published September 7, 2026

Case ID

KT-000007

Category

Workstation Decisions

Status

Replacement Recommended

Technologies

Windows / Scanner / Endpoint Performance

Problem

What happened?

A user reported that a desktop scanner and normal browser work were extremely slow or unreliable. The workstation was old enough to be a lifecycle concern, and the issue could not be evaluated as a scanner-only problem because the entire desktop experience was severely delayed.

An initial cleanup improved the browser and scanner enough to continue working, but CPU utilization remained at 100%. Within a few days, the same symptoms returned and the scanner software would no longer load or install cleanly.

Public-safe context

What was removed?

Customer identity

Organization, user, workstation, and contact information are omitted.

Device specifics

Exact hardware model, serial information, and private environment details are excluded.

Business data

Technical sequence

Investigation

How did a scanner ticket become a workstation decision?

Step 1

Start with the reported workflow

The visible symptom involved scanning, but browser performance was also extremely slow. That immediately widened the fault domain beyond one peripheral.

Step 2

Reduce obvious local pressure

Old profiles, temporary files, and stale local data were reviewed and partially cleaned up. After restart, the browser and scanner temporarily moved along much better.

Step 3

Measure what remained unhealthy

The workstation continued to show constant 100% CPU utilization even after the temporary improvement. The system was also roughly seven years old.

Step 4

Watch what happened after the repair

The improvement did not hold. Within days, the same performance problem returned, scanner software would not load properly, and normal interaction could take 10 to 20 seconds between clicks.

Decision

Why stop another repair cycle?

Step 5

Separate temporary recovery from durable health

The first cleanup proved that some local pressure could be reduced. It did not prove the workstation had returned to a trustworthy operating state because CPU saturation remained and the same workflow failed again quickly.

Step 6

Count technician time and user delay

When each click takes many seconds and even cleanup actions become slow, continued troubleshooting itself becomes part of the business cost.

Step 7

Choose replacement over another narrow fix

Once recurrence, age, constant CPU saturation, system-wide slowness, and scanner-software failure were considered together, a replacement workstation became the more defensible next step.

Step 8

Move into transition planning

A replacement quote was requested so the user could move to a newer workstation rather than continue spending support time on repeated temporary recovery.

Finding

What was actually proven?

The scanner symptom was not isolated from the workstation

The source record proves system-wide severe slowness, constant CPU saturation, recurring symptoms after temporary cleanup, failure of the scanner software to load or install normally, and an aging workstation.

The source supports a replacement recommendation and quote request. It does not prove that the replacement workstation was ultimately purchased, deployed, or that a single hardware component was the root cause.

Verification

The temporary repair was verified by retesting the browser and scanner after cleanup and restart. The larger workstation-health concern was verified by the persistent 100% CPU state and rapid recurrence of the same workflow problems.

The correct closure for this case is the engineering decision to stop another narrow repair cycle and move toward replacement planning, not a claim that a new computer had already been deployed.

Lessons Learned

Working again is not the same as worth repairing again.

A temporary improvement is not a durable repair when the platform remains saturated and the same workflow fails again days later.

Replacement decisions become stronger when age, repeat failure, system-wide performance, technician time, and recovery durability all point in the same direction.
